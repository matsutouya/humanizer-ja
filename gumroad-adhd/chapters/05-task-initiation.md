# Chapter 5 — The Two-Minute Lie: Task Initiation and How to Actually Start

## The wall nobody else can see

There's a task on your list. You know exactly what it is. You know how to do it. You even *want* it done — you can picture the relief of it being finished, the email sent, the form submitted, the dishes gone.

And you are physically not doing it.

You're doing something else — refilling your water, checking a feed you checked ninety seconds ago, tidying a drawer that has been untidy since 2019 — and the whole time there's a low hum of dread, because the task is still there, and you still can't start it.

If you've never experienced this, it sounds like laziness. If you have, you know it feels more like standing in front of a glass wall. You can see the task through it. You just can't get to it.

This chapter is about that wall. Chapter 1 promised we'd answer the engineering question — *"what would make starting feel like falling instead of climbing?"* — and this is where we pay that off. But first I want to demolish the most popular piece of starting advice on the internet, because it fails us in a very specific, very instructive way.

## Why "just do it for two minutes" is a lie (for us)

You've heard the two-minute rule: if a task takes less than two minutes, do it now, and for bigger tasks, just commit to two minutes of work — because starting is the hard part, and once you start, momentum carries you.

Here's the thing. The second half is actually true. Momentum *is* real; once we start, we're often fine, sometimes more than fine. The lie is the word "just."

"Just commit to two minutes" assumes starting costs everyone roughly the same small fee. It doesn't. For a typical brain, initiating a boring task is like stepping off a curb. For an ADHD brain, it can be like stepping off a curb that your nervous system, for reasons it declines to explain, has decided is a cliff.

Go back to the fuel system from chapter 1: our motivation runs on interest, novelty, challenge, and urgency — not importance. A boring-but-important task offers none of those fuels. So when you tell yourself "this will only take two minutes," you're making an importance-based argument to a motivation system that doesn't accept that currency. The task isn't hard. *Starting* is hard, because starting is the moment your brain has to pay the full activation cost up front, with no interest, no novelty, and no urgency to pay it with.

Chemists have a name for this: **activation energy.** Plenty of chemical reactions release energy overall — they *want* to happen, in a sense — but they still need an initial push to get going. Paper doesn't burst into flame on its own. It needs a match.

That's the reframe this whole chapter runs on. When you can't start, you don't have a laziness problem. You have an activation energy problem. And activation energy problems have engineering solutions: **you don't push harder on the reaction. You lower the energy required, or you add a catalyst.** Every technique below is one of those two moves.

One more thing before the techniques, because it matters: the freeze is not a character defect, and beating yourself up about it is worse than useless. Shame burns executive function — the exact resource starting requires. Every minute you spend on "what is wrong with me" is a minute of fuel the task will no longer get. Neutral spec-sheet language, remember: *"This brain has high activation costs for low-interest tasks."* Okay. Noted. Now let's engineer around it.

## Move one: shrink the doorway, not the room

The standard advice says "break the task into small steps." Fine as far as it goes, but it misses where the wall actually is. The wall isn't the whole task. The wall is the *first physical action* — and most of us have never defined it.

Look at your to-do list. I'll bet it says things like "do taxes," "email landlord," "clean kitchen." Those aren't tasks. Those are *projects wearing a task costume*, and your brain knows it. When you look at "do taxes," your brain instantly, invisibly runs the whole tree — find the login, which email did I use, where are those forms, didn't I have a freelance thing in March — and the activation energy of the entire tree lands on the moment of starting. No wonder you flinch.

The fix is to define an **entry point**: the first concrete, physical, thirty-second action, described so specifically that it requires zero decisions.

- Not "do taxes" → **"open the folder called Taxes and look at it."** That's it. That's the whole task.
- Not "email landlord" → **"open a blank email and type the landlord's name in the To field."**
- Not "clean kitchen" → **"put one glass in the dishwasher."**

I know how this sounds. It sounds like cheating, or like coddling yourself. Here's why it isn't: **decisions are the expensive part.** Executive function drains fastest on choosing, sequencing, and holding steps in your head. An entry point pre-pays all of that the night before, or the minute you write the list — when you're calm and the task is *not now* — so that at the moment of starting, there's nothing left to decide. You're not asking your brain to start a project. You're asking it to perform one dumb physical motion.

And then momentum, the one true part of the two-minute rule, takes over. Not always — sometimes you put one glass in the dishwasher and stop, and that's a legitimate outcome, one glass better than zero. But a remarkable percentage of the time, the person holding the glass looks at the counter and thinks "well, I'm here now." Starting was the whole war. You just won it with a glass.

## Move two: lower the wall the night before

Activation energy isn't fixed. It rises and falls with friction, and friction is something you can manipulate when the task is safely *not now*.

The principle: **make the desired action the laziest available option.**

- Want to go for a run in the morning? The decision doesn't happen at 7 a.m. It happens the night before, when you lay the clothes on the chair, shoes by the door. Morning-you doesn't decide to run. Morning-you just puts on the outfit that's inexplicably already assembled.
- Need to write tomorrow? Leave the document *open on the screen*, cursor mid-sentence. (Hemingway allegedly stopped mid-sentence on purpose. Whatever else you think of Hemingway, that's an ADHD-grade entry point.)
- Dreading a phone call? Put the number, the account reference, and a three-bullet script on a sticky note tonight. Tomorrow the call is a reading exercise, not a task.

The mirror-image move is raising the wall for the things that eat you. If your phone is the black hole, the engineering answer isn't "resist the phone" — chapter 1 already told you what willpower is worth. It's "phone charges in the other room while I work." You're not stronger than the phone. You're just no longer in the same room as it, and for once, the activation energy problem works *for* you: walking to another room is a wall, and now it's protecting you.

## Move three: add fuel the tank actually accepts

Sometimes the doorway is tiny, the friction is gone, and you still can't start, because the task offers none of the four fuels — interest, novelty, challenge, urgency. Fine. Add some artificially.

**Manufactured urgency.** Deadlines work on us; distant ones don't, because of the *now / not now* thing from chapter 1. So make deadlines near and loud. Race a timer: "how much of this inbox can I clear before the kettle boils?" Book something *after* the task — schedule the boring admin for 3:00 when a friend is calling at 3:45. The container creates the urgency the task couldn't.

**Manufactured novelty.** Do the task somewhere weird. Boring report? Coffee shop, or the floor, or standing at the kitchen counter. Fold laundry to a true-crime podcast you're only allowed to hear while folding laundry — pairing a dopamine source with a dead task is one of the oldest tricks in the ADHD playbook because it works.

**Manufactured challenge.** Turn the task into a game with rules only you know. Type the email without touching the mouse. Beat yesterday's time. Dumb? Extremely. But your motivation system doesn't grade on dignity. It grades on interest, and "arbitrary self-imposed game" is, embarrassingly, interesting.

**Borrowed presence.** The single most reliable catalyst I know is another human being in the room, or on a screen, doing their own work while you do yours. It's strong enough that it gets its own chapter — that's chapter 7, body doubling. For now just file it: when nothing on this list works, a person will.

## When the wall is actually a message

A caution, because I promised you no toxic positivity.

Sometimes you can't start because of activation energy. But sometimes you can't start because some part of you has correctly noticed something the rest of you hasn't admitted: the task is ambiguous ("what does 'sort out insurance' even *mean*?"), or it's scary (starting means finding out how bad the bank balance is), or it genuinely shouldn't be done at all (a commitment you should quit rather than start).

If you've shrunk the doorway, killed the friction, added fuel, and the wall is still there after several honest attempts — stop pushing and interrogate the task instead. Ask: *what's the actual first question I don't know the answer to?* and *what am I afraid I'll find out?* Ambiguity has an engineering fix (define the entry point at the level of the ambiguity: "text Sam and ask what 'sort out insurance' means"). Fear sometimes needs a friend on the phone while you open the scary letter. And a task you've failed to start forty times might be your wiring casting a veto worth listening to — chapter 9 has a lot more to say about that.

The freeze is data. Read it before you fight it.

## The starting ritual, assembled

Here's how the pieces snap together into something you can run daily. Mine looks like this; adjust the parts to your life.

1. **The night before (or end of workday):** pick tomorrow's one ugly task. Write its entry point — first physical action, thirty seconds, zero decisions. Stage the environment: materials out, document open, distractions physically elsewhere.
2. **At start time:** don't negotiate. Negotiating with yourself at the moment of starting always goes badly, because the debater and the judge are the same tired brain. Instead, run a launch sequence — same song, same drink, same chair, then the entry point. Rituals work because they replace a decision with a habit, and habits are cheap.
3. **Do the entry point.** Just the entry point. You have full permission to stop after it.
4. **If momentum shows up, ride it.** If it doesn't, you still started, which means the day's hardest rep is done. One glass in the dishwasher is not a failure state. It's a foothold, and tomorrow starts from a foothold instead of a wall.

Notice what's absent from that sequence: willpower, self-talk, "just," and any requirement to feel motivated. Motivation, for brains like ours, is usually a *result* of starting, not a prerequisite. We don't wait to feel like it. We build a ramp, and we fall down it.

That's the whole trick, and it's the sentence I'd tattoo on this chapter if chapters had forearms: **stop trying to climb into tasks. Engineer them so you can fall in.**

### 📦 Try this today

Pick the one task you've been avoiding longest — the one that made you slightly sick to think of just now. Don't do it. Instead, spend ninety seconds writing its **entry point**: the first physical action, doable in thirty seconds, requiring zero decisions. ("Open the folder and look at it" is a perfectly legal answer.) Then stage it for tomorrow morning — document open, papers out, sticky note on the laptop.

Tomorrow, do only the entry point. Stopping afterward is allowed. Notice what actually happens once you're standing inside the task instead of in front of it.
