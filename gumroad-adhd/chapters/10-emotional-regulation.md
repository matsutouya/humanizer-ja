# Chapter 10 — Emotional Regulation and Rejection Sensitivity at Work

This is the chapter I'd most like to hand to the version of me who once spent an entire weekend replaying a four-word comment from a Thursday meeting.

The comment was "let's discuss this offline." That's it. That's the whole comment. A neutral scheduling remark, made by a manager who, I later learned, had liked the proposal. My brain heard it as a public verdict — *this is bad, you've embarrassed yourself, everyone saw* — and produced a physical wave of heat and dread that lasted through Saturday. By Monday I had mentally drafted my resignation twice.

If that story sounds unhinged to you, you can probably skim this chapter. If it sounds like Tuesday — if you know exactly what that heat feels like — then welcome. This one's ours.

Quick reminder of the ground rules from chapter 1: I'm writing from lived experience and research-informed self-help, not from a clinician's chair. Emotional regulation is the area of ADHD where professional support matters most, and I'll say so specifically as we go. What I can offer is the engineering layer — the protocols and scripts that make workdays survivable while you do whatever deeper work you and a professional decide on.

## The missing chapter of the ADHD story

When you got your mental model in chapter 1 — regulation of attention, motivation, time — there was a fourth word in that list that most public conversation about ADHD skips: **emotion.**

ADHD isn't just difficulty regulating what you pay attention to. For many of us, it's difficulty regulating how hard feelings hit and how long they last before the volume comes back down. The feelings themselves aren't abnormal — everyone dislikes criticism, everyone gets frustrated. What's different is the *gain* on the amplifier and the missing damping. A typical brain gets criticized and feels a manageable sting that fades over an hour. Ours can get the full flood — instant, physical, out of proportion to the trigger, and slow to drain.

The specific flavor of this that dominates work life has a name you may have run into: **rejection sensitive dysphoria**, or RSD. A quick honesty note: RSD isn't a formal diagnosis you'll find in the diagnostic manuals — it's a term that emerged from clinical observation and got adopted by the community at scale because it named something thousands of us instantly recognized. I use it here as a description of a common lived experience, not a medical claim. And the experience it describes is this: real or *perceived* rejection and criticism produce emotional pain that is sudden, physical, and wildly disproportionate — closer to injury than to disappointment.

Perceived is the load-bearing word. The email with no exclamation point. The meeting you weren't cc'd on. The "fine." The two-day silence after you submitted the draft. An RSD-flavored brain reads ambiguity as rejection by default, feels the injury *before* any conscious thought happens, and then — because our brains are nothing if not thorough — hyperfocuses on the wound for hours or days.

Here's why this belongs in a careers book and not just a feelings book: **unmanaged rejection sensitivity quietly makes your career decisions for you.** It's why you didn't apply for the promotion (application = exposure to rejection). Why you don't ask clarifying questions (might look stupid). Why you either people-please until you burn out or pre-emptively quit things before they can reject you first. Some of us armor up as perfectionists — if it's flawless, it can't be criticized — which for an ADHD brain means projects that never ship. The tax isn't just pain. It's all the swings never taken.

And one more cost, the one nobody warns you about: **the reaction to the reaction.** You feel the flood, and then you feel ashamed of the flood — *why am I like this, it was four words* — and the shame extends the episode far past the original sting. That's the same vicious loop from chapter 1, running on emotion instead of tasks.

## Reframe first, then engineer

The move here is the same one we've made all book, and it matters more here than anywhere.

The deficit story says: you're too sensitive, you're unprofessional, you take everything personally, grow thicker skin. Twenty years of "grow thicker skin" has probably worked about as well as twenty years of "just use a planner," and for the same reason — it's willpower advice aimed at hardware.

The operating-system story says: **your brain has an oversensitive smoke alarm.** It's not detecting fake smoke — the feeling is real, the pain is real. It's detecting *candles* as *house fires*. You can't uninstall the alarm. But you can do two things with an oversensitive alarm: build a protocol for when it goes off, so you stop treating every alert as a verified fire — and stop being ashamed of the alarm, because shame is what turns a false alarm into a lost weekend.

One boundary before the protocols, and I mean it: if emotional flooding is regularly costing you jobs or relationships, if the crashes look like depression, or if you're using substances to quiet the alarm — that's past the self-help waterline. A therapist who understands ADHD can offer real tools for exactly this, and for many people medication meaningfully changes the amplifier's gain — a conversation for you and a prescriber, not for me. Everything below works *alongside* professional support, not instead of it. Getting that support is left-handed scissors, chapter 1 rules. No speech required.

## The pause protocol: engineering for the flood

Here's the most important mechanical fact about emotional flooding: **the flood peaks and recedes.** The unbearable intensity of minute two is not the intensity of minute twenty. Which means the entire game is what you do — and crucially, what you *don't* do — inside that window.

The failure mode is acting at the peak. The defensive reply-all. The "actually, let me explain" that digs deeper. The resignation fantasy that leaks into a real conversation. Peak-flood decisions are made by the alarm, not by you, and they convert a private false alarm into a public, permanent fact.

So we build a pause protocol — and we build it *in advance, on a calm day*, because designing a fire escape during the fire is not a plan. Mine has four steps; adapt freely.

**Step 1: Name it mechanically.** The moment you feel the heat — and it is physical; learn your tells (face heat, stomach drop, the sudden urge to defend or disappear) — say the script, silently or out loud: *"This is the alarm. It's loud, and it might be wrong."* You're not suppressing the feeling or arguing with it. You're labeling it, which creates the first millimeter of gap between the feeling and you. That millimeter is everything.

**Step 2: No output for a set time.** Pick your number in advance — mine is two hours for anything digital, overnight for anything big. During the window: no sending, no posting, no confronting, no deciding. Write the furious reply if you must — in a doc with the recipient field *empty* — but nothing leaves the building. This isn't repression; the message can still be sent later. It's just a rule that the alarm doesn't get to hold the send button. If it helps, externalize it chapter-3 style: an actual sticky note on the monitor that says "flooded = no send."

**Step 3: Move the body, change the room.** Flooding is physiological, so answer it physiologically. Walk, stairs, cold water on the wrists, ninety seconds of slow exhale-heavy breathing, step outside. This isn't wellness garnish — you're helping the wave recede faster, and physically leaving the context breaks the hyperfocus loop that wants to replay the trigger forever.

**Step 4: Reality-check with one calibrated human.** After the peak, not before: run the trigger past one trusted person. Not a sympathy-only friend and not a harsh one — someone calibrated. "Manager said 'let's discuss offline' — reading this as bad. Am I?" Nine times out of ten, an outside brain spots the candle immediately. This is chapter 7's borrowed executive function, applied to emotion — a body double for your judgment. If you have a colleague-friend with ADHD, trade this service both ways; you can each see through the other's smoke instantly, even while blind in your own.

Then, only after all four: respond, if a response is even needed. You'll find that the message you send at hour three is consistently — embarrassingly — better than the one you drafted at minute two. Collect a few of those before-and-after pairs. They become personal, undeniable evidence that the pause works, which makes the next pause easier to choose.

## Scripts: feedback conversations without the flood

Protocols handle ambushes. But some rejection-shaped events are *scheduled* — performance reviews, design critiques, "got a minute?" Slacks — and scheduled events can be engineered in advance. Words fail us mid-flood, so we script them before it. Steal any of these:

**Shaping how feedback reaches you.** Format changes are cheap for the giver and enormous for you:

- *"Could you send written notes before we meet? I give you a much better conversation when I've processed first."* — This one is gold. It moves your first exposure to the criticism into private, where you can flood, run the protocol, and arrive at the meeting on the far side of the wave.
- *"Can we start with what's working before the fixes? I calibrate better with both data points."* — Framed as calibration, because it is. This is engineering, not fishing.
- In the room, buying seconds: *"Let me think about that for a second"* — full sentence, totally professional, and it buys the gap that step 1 needs. Also: writing notes while receiving criticism gives your hands a job and your eyes somewhere neutral to be.

**Responding before you've metabolized it.** For when you're asked to react on the spot and the alarm is mid-shriek:

- *"That's useful — I want to give it real thought rather than a knee-jerk answer. Can I come back to you tomorrow?"* — This reads as maturity, because it is maturity. Nobody has ever been fired for it.
- If you flooded visibly and need to repair: *"I came across defensive earlier — the feedback was fair and I wanted a redo on my reaction."* — Short, no self-flagellation, done. People respect this far more than the flawless composure you were failing to fake.

**Checking perceived rejection instead of stewing.** The alternative to a weekend of replaying is thirty seconds of asking:

- *"Quick check — your note on the draft read a bit terse to me. All good, or is there a concern I should know about?"* — Low-drama, answerable, and it converts three days of imagined verdicts into one minute of actual data. The answer is almost always "oh — no, I was just in a hurry."

A word on disclosure, since these scripts flirt with it: you never owe anyone your diagnosis, and none of these scripts require it. "I process feedback best in writing" is a work-style statement, full stop. Whether to disclose further is a personal, situational call — some workplaces reward that trust and some punish it, and pretending otherwise would violate this book's no-toxic-positivity clause. Share the spec, not necessarily the schematic.

## The long game: draining the backlog

Protocols and scripts manage episodes. The deeper shift is slower, and it's the same one this book has been making all along.

Every time you run the pause and discover the fire was a candle, you're logging evidence against the alarm's calibration. Every time you ask the checking question and get "no, all good," the ambiguity-means-rejection default weakens slightly. Every repair script that lands teaches you that a visible flood is survivable — which, quietly, is the thing RSD never let you believe. None of this is fast. The smoke alarm was calibrated by decades of the deficit story — every "so much potential" conversation taught it that judgment is always incoming. It doesn't recalibrate in a month.

But it recalibrates. Slowly, with reps, with support, the gap between trigger and flood widens, the floods shorten, and one day you notice a "let's discuss offline" and feel only a normal, boring flicker of uncertainty — and then you just *ask*. That's the whole aspiration, honestly. Not thick skin. Accurate smoke detection, and a fire escape you've practiced enough to trust.

Your sensitivity, by the way, is on both lists from chapter 1 — same wiring both directions. The brain that over-reads a terse email is the brain that reads a room instantly, notices the colleague who's quietly drowning, and brings intensity to everything it loves. You don't get to return one and keep the other. You just learn where each one wins.

### 📦 Try this today

Build your pause protocol *now*, while nothing is on fire, and externalize it — a note on your phone titled "ALARM":

1. **My tells:** the two or three physical signs that mean I'm flooding.
2. **My script:** "This is the alarm. It's loud, and it might be wrong."
3. **My rule:** no sending, deciding, or confronting for ___ hours. (Pick the number now.)
4. **My person:** the one calibrated human I'll reality-check with — text them today and actually ask: "When I ping you with 'am I over-reading this?', tell me straight."

Optional but powerful: send one format-request script this week — *"Any chance you could send written notes before our next review? I do my best thinking with a head start."* One sentence, sent on a calm day, that quietly redesigns every difficult conversation you'll ever have there. That's the whole method of this book in miniature: build the ramp before you need it.
