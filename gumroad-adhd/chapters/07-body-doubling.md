# Chapter 7 — Body Doubling, Accountability, and Borrowing Other People's Executive Function

## The librarian effect

Here's a small mystery from my own life that took me embarrassingly long to notice.

At home, alone, with total freedom and every tool I could want, I could sit down to do admin work and produce forty minutes of tab-opening, snack-fetching, and light self-loathing. Same task, same laptop, in a library or a coffee shop — surrounded by strangers who did not know me, were not watching me, and could not have cared less what I did — and the work just... happened. Quietly. Almost easily.

Nobody was checking on me. Nobody would have said a word if I'd watched videos for three hours. And yet the presence of other humans, doing their own things, changed what my brain was able to do. For years I filed this under "I guess I like coffee shops" and moved on.

Then I learned it has a name — **body doubling** — and that it's one of the most consistently reported tricks in the entire ADHD playbook. The version with a name is even simpler than my accidental version: you do your boring task while another person is present, in the room or on a video call, usually doing their *own* boring task. They don't help you. They don't supervise you. They just exist nearby, and somehow that's enough to melt the glass wall from chapter 5.

Chapter 5 ended with a promise: when the entry points and the staged environments and the manufactured urgency all fail, a person will work. This chapter is that person. More broadly, it's about the single most underrated resource available to an ADHD brain: **other people's executive function**, and how to borrow it without shame, without becoming a burden, and without pretending you don't need it.

## Why a silent stranger outperforms your willpower

Nobody has a complete explanation for why body doubling works, and I promised not to play neuroscientist. But the practical mechanics are easy to observe, and they map neatly onto the fuel system from chapter 1.

**Presence creates a gentle, ambient urgency.** Not deadline panic — more like a soft social gravity. Some ancient part of your brain notes that another human is here and working, and quietly concludes that this is a time and place where working is what's done. Importance couldn't get your motivation system to produce fuel; mild social context, absurdly, can.

**It adds stakes without adding pressure.** The magic of a body double, as opposed to a supervisor, is that the stakes are almost nothing. If you drift, nothing happens. There's no report card. But *you'd notice*, in front of another person, and that whisper of witness is often exactly the right dose. Enough to hold the channel open; not enough to trigger the anxiety that shuts us down.

**It shrinks the decision space.** Alone, every moment secretly contains a question: keep working, or...? Anything else in the world? A body-doubling session has a frame — we're here, until 2:00, doing our tasks — and frames are cheap substitutes for the self-regulation we're short on. The session decides, so you don't have to.

**It borrows regulation.** This is the deepest one. Sitting near a calm, focused person, you tend to sync toward calm and focused. Parents of small children know this trick from the other side: a kid who "can't" do homework alone often can with a parent silently reading nearby. The parent isn't teaching. The parent is *lending regulation*. We never grow out of benefiting from this. We just grow into being embarrassed about it, which brings me to the flag I want to plant before we get tactical.

**Needing this is not a failure.** The deficit story will whisper that real adults work independently, that needing company to answer emails is childish. Notice that nobody says this about spotters at the gym, or study groups, or the entire concept of an office. Neurotypical people build their lives around borrowed structure and call it "culture." We do it deliberately and call it a crutch. Left-handed scissors, one more time: it's an accommodation, it's unremarkable, and the results speak. You don't get extra credit for doing life on hard mode.

## The body doubling menu

There are more ways to do this than people realize. Steal liberally; combine freely.

**The classic: one friend, side by side.** You bring your taxes; they bring their emails. Same table, mostly silent, maybe a check-in when you both surface. Works in person or over a lazy video call where nobody performs anything. This is also the humane answer to scary tasks — the letter you can't open, the bill you can't face. You don't need someone to fix it. You need someone in the room while you look at it, because dread divided by two is usually small enough to start.

**Coworking spaces, cafés, libraries.** Ambient body doubling with strangers — my accidental version. Zero coordination required, which makes it the lowest-friction option. Bonus: leaving the house to work adds a commitment device (you came all this way) and strips your home distractions of their gravity automatically. If your work allows it, "I do my worst tasks at the library" is a complete, respectable system.

**Focusmate-style matched sessions.** There are services built exactly for this — Focusmate is the best-known — that pair you with a stranger for a timed video session, typically 25 to 75 minutes. The format is beautifully rigid: you join, you each state your goal out loud in thirty seconds ("clearing my inbox"; "writing a lab report"), you mute and work with cameras on, and at the end you each report how it went. That's it. You will never see this person again. It should not work. It works *stunningly* — for many of us, more reliably than working with friends, because a stranger plus an appointment plus a stated goal stacks three catalysts at once: the session is a hard start time (manufactured urgency), the stated goal is a commitment, and the camera is a witness. Booking one is also a world-class entry point in the chapter 5 sense: at 9:58 you're a person who can't start; at 10:00 the session starts *for* you.

**Online coworking rooms and streams.** Standing video rooms run by ADHD communities, "study with me" streams where thousands of people work alongside a stranger and a timer, Discord servers with a permanent "focus room" voice channel. The parasocial version is weaker than a live human but astonishingly better than nothing, and it's available at 2 a.m. when nothing else is.

**The household double.** Don't overlook the person you live with. "Will you sit with me while I do this?" is a complete sentence, and most partners, roommates, and friends are *glad* to be asked — it's a low-effort way to be genuinely helpful, and it usually gets their own annoying task done too. Folding laundry together while one of you also sorts the mail is body doubling. It doesn't need a name to count.

A note on choosing: match the double to the task. Chatty friends are wonderful and terrible body doubles — if the friendship is more interesting than the task, the task loses. For truly dreaded work, I want structure and near-strangers. For long grinding work, ambient café presence. For scary-envelope work, one trusted person who knows what they're there for.

## Beyond presence: accountability structures

Body doubling handles the hour of work in front of you. Accountability structures handle the week and the month — the projects that die between sessions because nobody, including you, would notice.

The design principle for all of them: **an external, scheduled, human checkpoint converts "importance" (which our fuel system ignores) into "social urgency" (which it runs on beautifully).** Some formats, in ascending order of commitment:

**The declared goal.** Cheapest option: tell one specific person one specific thing. "I'm sending the invoice by Friday — ask me about it Saturday." The entire structure is one text message, and the thought of Saturday's question generates more fuel than the invoice's actual importance ever did. Use it for one-offs.

**The accountability partner.** A standing, recurring exchange — weekly call, or even just a scheduled text thread: what I said last week, what actually happened, what I'm claiming for next week. Two rules make or break it. First, *symmetry*: it must run both ways, or it curdles into supervision and you'll start dodging it. Second, *no-shame reporting*: the answer "I didn't do any of it" must be a boring data point, not a confession. The moment reporting failure feels like standing in the principal's office, your rejection-sensitive brain (chapter 10 is coming) will protect you by avoiding your partner, and the structure dies. Agree on this explicitly, out loud, at the start: we are collecting data, not grading each other.

**Groups, gyms, and cohorts.** Classes with fixed times, group challenges, cohort-based courses — anything where the structure exists whether or not you show up, and your absence is visible. Expensive versions (a trainer, a course with live sessions) buy something real: you're paying money specifically to convert importance into appointments. For an ADHD brain that is often a genuinely good trade, not an indulgence.

**Professional scaffolding.** Coaches — including ADHD coaches specifically — sit at the top of the ladder: a recurring appointment with a person whose whole job is being your externalized planning-and-review function. Not everyone needs this or can afford it. But if your executive function shortfall is costing you real money and real opportunities, hiring some is a legitimate line item, exactly as unremarkable as hiring an accountant because tax law isn't your specialty.

Two warnings from the school of expensive experience. **Deadlines with faces beat deadlines with dates** — "the report is due Friday" is weightless; "Priya is expecting it Friday" has mass; build your commitments accordingly, and be honest with yourself about the difference. And **don't build a structure you'll be punished by.** If you're in a low season, a heavy accountability system becomes one more thing to fail at in front of witnesses. Scale it to reality: sometimes the right structure for the month is one Focusmate session and one kind friend, and that's not quitting, that's engineering to load.

## Borrowing without burning out the lender

One more thing, because it's the part that keeps this sustainable for decades instead of months.

If one person — usually a partner — becomes your only body double, your only reminder system, your only deadline-with-a-face, the borrowing stops being charming and starts being a job they never applied for. The relationship tilts from partners toward manager-and-managed, and that tilt breeds resentment on both sides. You've maybe felt the early wobble of it already.

The fix isn't to stop borrowing. It's to **diversify and to be explicit.** Spread the load across the whole menu: strangers for sessions, apps and alarms for reminders (chapters 3 and 4 exist precisely so humans don't have to do a robot's job), a peer for weekly accountability, and your partner for the small subset only a partner can do. Ask, don't assume — "would you be up for sitting with me Sunday mornings while I do the finance stuff?" is a request that can be declined, which is what makes yes mean something. Say thank you like you mean it, because being someone's calm in the room is real work even when it looks like reading a book. And return the loan in your own currency: your crisis-calm, your hyperfocus aimed at their project, your creative firepower. Chapter 1's list one exists for a reason. Borrowing executive function only feels shameful if you're pretending the ledger runs one way. It never does.

Alone, in a quiet room, with nothing but willpower — that's the environment where our operating system posts its worst numbers, and it's the exact environment modern life keeps handing us. You now know it's a lie that you have to accept it. Other people are an environmental modification. The best one there is.

### 📦 Try this today

Book one body-doubling session for this week — pick whichever has the least friction for you: a Focusmate-style matched session, a coffee-shop hour with a friend ("bring your own boring task"), or asking whoever you live with to sit nearby while you handle the thing you've been dodging. Assign the session a task from your list *before* it starts, entry point and all.

Then notice — honestly — how the same task felt with a human nearby versus alone. If the difference is as big as I think it'll be, you've just found the cheapest productivity infrastructure you will ever own.
