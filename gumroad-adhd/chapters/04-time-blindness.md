# Chapter 4 — Time Blindness: Making Time Visible, Physical, and Loud

## "I'll leave in five minutes," said at 6:40, meant sincerely, repeated at 7:15

Here's a scene from my life that I'm choosing to share because I know it's a scene from yours.

I need to leave at 7:00. At 6:40 I check the time and feel a small glow of competence — twenty whole minutes, plenty of time, I'm actually early for once. I decide to do one small thing while I wait. Just one. Rinse a dish, answer a message, look up one fact.

The next time I experience the concept of time, it's 7:15. Not gradually — *suddenly*, the way you surface from deep water. Thirty-five minutes have passed and I have no felt sense of them passing. I'm not late because I don't care. I'm late because between 6:40 and 7:15, time simply didn't exist for me. It wasn't running in the background. There is no background.

The name for this is **time blindness**, and it might be the single most misunderstood part of ADHD, because from the outside it looks exactly like disrespect. Chronically late people are read as people who think their time matters more than yours. Meanwhile, from the inside, you're a person who checked the clock with genuinely good intentions and then got mugged by a missing half hour.

In chapter 1, I described the ADHD experience of time as two zones: *now* and *not now*. This chapter is where we deal with the practical wreckage of that — the lateness, the blown deadlines, the "how is it already 4 p.m.," the tasks that were going to take twenty minutes and took four hours — and build the fix. And the fix follows the same engineering logic as everything else in this book, especially chapter 3's core move: **what your brain can't hold internally, you build externally.** We externalized memory. Now we externalize time.

The strategy has three parts, right there in the chapter title: make time **visible**, make it **physical**, and make it **loud**.

## What's actually broken (and what isn't)

Quick model, so the fixes make sense.

Most people carry an internal clock that runs continuously, even when they're absorbed in something. It's imprecise but present — a background hum of "about twenty minutes have passed" and "the deadline is getting closer." That hum is what lets someone cook dinner while watching a show and still stir the pot roughly on time.

For many ADHD brains, that background process barely runs. Time perception isn't degraded so much as *intermittent* — it exists when you're actively looking at a clock and vanishes the moment attention lands anywhere else. And attention landing hard on things is, as we established in chapter 2, kind of our whole deal. Hyperfocus is the extreme case: hours pass in what feels like minutes, and no internal alarm ever sounds.

The same missing hum explains the other classic symptoms:

- **Estimation is guesswork.** "How long will this take?" requires an internal database of felt durations, and ours is corrupt. So everything is "about twenty minutes" — the shower, the errand, the report — and everything is wrong in both directions.
- **Future time has no weight.** A deadline in three weeks is *not now*, filed next to "someday," generating zero urgency fuel until it violently teleports into *now* (this is chapter 2's urgency addiction, seen from the other side).
- **Transitions ambush you.** Without the hum counting down, every "time to stop and switch" arrives as a surprise, and surprises get resisted.

Here's the part I need you to actually absorb, not just nod at: **you cannot fix this by caring more.** You already care. You've cared for years, and you're still late. Caring is pressure, and — say it with me — pressure is not fuel, and it's also not a clock. The fix isn't internal. The fix is prosthetic, same as chapter 3: since the internal clock doesn't hum, we surround you with external ones that do.

## Make time visible: the case for analog

Start with the strangest, most reliable trick in the time-blindness toolkit: **get an analog clock.** A big, boring, round one with hands, hung where you actually live your life — kitchen, office wall, above the desk. Yes, in this century.

Here's why it works when your phone doesn't. A digital clock tells you a *fact*: 6:42. Facts get read and discarded. An analog clock shows you a *shape*: you can see the slice of space between the minute hand and 7:00, see it as an amount, watch it shrink. Digital time is a number you have to do math on. Analog time is a picture of how much is left. For a brain that doesn't feel time, a picture of time is the closest available substitute — it routes around the broken sense entirely and borrows a working one: your eyes.

The same principle scales down to the desk. **Visual timers** — the kind where a colored disk shrinks as time runs out — do for a single task what the wall clock does for the hour. Watching a red wedge get thinner *is* feeling time pass, outsourced to vision. They're sold as tools for kids' classrooms, which stops a lot of adults from buying one, which is a shame, because the classroom kids and we have the same missing hum. Get the adult-looking one if the pride matters. Get one either way.

And there's a phone-specific warning hiding in here: the phone is the worst possible clock, because checking the time on it means *picking up the portal to everything interesting in the world*. You went in for the time; you came out twenty minutes later, informed about many things, none of them the time. Time-checking needs to be glanceable, ambient, and boring — a wall, a wrist, a desk. Never a feed.

## Make time loud: alarms as bumpers, not verdicts

Visibility handles the time you're willing to look at. For the time that passes while you're absorbed, you need sound — because sound is the one channel that reaches into hyperfocus from outside.

The move here isn't "set an alarm." You've set alarms. The move is a specific pattern I think of as **bumpers**: multiple alarms bracketing a transition, each with a *named job*.

Take the 7:00 departure. One alarm at 7:00 is useless — by the time it rings, being ready is no longer possible, so the alarm is just a notification that you've failed, and your brain learns to hate and then ignore it. Instead:

- **6:30 — "Last new thing."** Nothing gets *started* after this. Not one dish, not one email. Starting things is the gateway drug of lateness.
- **6:45 — "Shoes and bag."** A concrete physical action, not a status update. Alarms that command a body movement work; alarms that convey information get dismissed.
- **6:55 — "Walk out."** You're leaving now, in whatever state you're in.

Label them exactly like that in your phone, because future-you, mid-hyperfocus, will not remember what the 6:45 alarm was for, and an unlabeled alarm gets swiped away like a mosquito. A labeled alarm is an instruction from someone who loves you.

Two refinements that took me years to learn:

**Alarms are for compliance, not consideration.** When the "shoes and bag" alarm fires, the deal is: you obey it like a fire drill, without re-litigating. The moment you allow "just let me finish this one thing," you've converted the alarm from a bumper back into a suggestion, and your brain files suggestions under *not now*. The whole value is that the decision was made by past-you, calmly, in advance — present-you just executes. (If you notice you've started negotiating with a particular alarm regularly, don't add discipline. Change the sound. Novelty, chapter 2 — an alarm tone you've heard four hundred times becomes wallpaper.)

**Ring the beginnings, not just the ends.** Most people alarm deadlines. We need alarms for *starts*: "start getting ready," "start wrapping up," "start dinner or tonight becomes cereal." The end of a time window was never our problem — we've met plenty of deadlines at the exact last second. It's the beginning that silently escapes.

## Time anchors: hanging the day on things that actually happen

Alarms structure minutes. For structuring a whole day, the tool is the **time anchor**: a fixed, external, preferably other-people-involving event that happens at a known time, which the rest of the day can hang from.

Here's the problem anchors solve. A wide-open day — the freelancer's Tuesday, the empty Saturday — sounds like freedom and behaves like a swamp. With no fixed points, every hour is interchangeable, so nothing is urgent, so nothing starts, and the day dissolves into a smear of almost-doing-things. You've had that day. It doesn't feel like freedom afterward.

An anchor breaks the swamp. A 9:30 gym class, a standing coffee with a friend, a daily check-in call, even a scheduled show — suddenly the day has a *shape*. There's "before the anchor" and "after the anchor," and each is small enough to plan. Better yet, remember the observation from chapter 2 that some of us focus best in the 90 minutes before an immovable commitment? Anchors manufacture those windows on purpose. Two anchors a day — one morning, one afternoon — can turn a swamp into three usable rooms.

The load-bearing word is *external*. "I'll start work at 9" is an anchor made of intentions, and we both know its tensile strength. An anchor holds in proportion to how awkward it is to miss — a class you paid for, a person who'll be standing there, a call someone else dialed. If your days keep dissolving, the fix usually isn't a better schedule. It's one or two real anchors, involving other humans, placed like tent poles.

## Backward planning: the only estimation method that survives contact with us

Last tool, and it's the one that quietly fixes lateness at the root.

Time-blind planning runs forward from now: "It's 6:00, the dinner's at 7:30, I have loads of time." Every step gets the corrupt-database estimate ("shower, like, ten minutes"), nothing includes transitions, and the total is a fantasy.

**Backward planning** runs the other way. Start at the fixed point and walk home:

- Dinner at 7:30 → need to arrive 7:25.
- Drive is 25 minutes (checked the map, not vibes) → leave at 7:00.
- Shoes-coat-keys-wallet-double-back-for-the-thing: 10 minutes, because it is always 10 minutes and never 2 → start leaving at 6:50.
- Shower and dress: honestly 30, not "like ten" → start at 6:20.
- Therefore the *real* deadline tonight is 6:20 — and 6:20 gets the bumper alarms, because 6:20 is the moment the evening is actually won or lost. By 7:00 the outcome was decided forty minutes ago.

Do this on paper or in your notes app the first several times, not in your head — working memory can't hold a five-step chain, as chapter 3 established at length. And while you're writing, apply the two corrections that cover most of the estimation gap: **count the transitions** (getting into and out of every activity is a step that takes real minutes and appears in no one's mental plan), and **use observed numbers, not felt ones**. You don't know how long your shower takes; you know how long it *feels*, which is fiction. Time a few of your routine tasks once — actually time them — and the numbers will genuinely surprise you. That little list of true durations is a permanent upgrade to every plan you'll ever make.

Backward planning works for deadlines too, and this is where it connects back to chapter 2's urgency problem. "Report due in three weeks" is *not now* and produces nothing. Walked backward — final read-through Thursday, draft done Monday, outline this Friday, therefore *reading starts today* — the far deadline becomes a chain of near ones, and near deadlines you can feel. Attach a witness to a couple of the links (send the outline to a colleague Friday) and you've manufactured chapter 2's early-urgency doses right where the timeline needs them.

## You're not going to become punctual. You're going to become armored.

Let me set expectations honestly, because this book doesn't do miracle endings. The internal clock isn't going to start humming. Twenty years from now, absorbed in something wonderful, you will still be capable of losing an afternoon without noticing — that's the hardware, and it's the same hardware that makes the absorption possible at all.

What changes is the wreckage. A wall you can glance at, a wedge of red disk shrinking beside your laptop, three labeled alarms guarding every departure, a couple of human-shaped anchors holding each day open, and a five-line backward plan for anything that matters — none of these require you to feel time. They feel it for you, and they nag you at the right moments, which is all a clock ever did for anyone.

People will start describing you as someone who's "gotten it together." You'll know the truth: you didn't get it together. You built scaffolding, bolted it to the outside of the day, and let it hold what your head was never going to hold. As we covered in chapter 1 — that's not cheating. That's left-handed scissors.

Next chapter, we deal with the moment all this scaffolding delivers you to: the task is in front of you, the time is now, everything is ready — and you still can't start. Task initiation is its own beast, and it deserves its own chapter.

### 📦 Try this today

Two moves, twenty minutes total.

First: order or dig out one **visual clock** — an analog wall clock for the room you lose the most time in, or a visual countdown timer for your desk. (If it's buried in a closet, hanging it counts as the whole task.)

Second: take your next real departure — tomorrow's work leave-time, an appointment, anything with a fixed clock time — and **plan it backward on paper**. Fixed point at the top, walk home step by step, count the transitions, use honest numbers. Find the real deadline (the 6:20, not the 7:00), and set three labeled bumper alarms on it: *last new thing*, *physical action*, *walk out*.

Then notice, tomorrow, what it feels like to leave on time without anyone having tried harder.
