# Chapter 8 — Money and ADHD: Impulse Spending, Invisible Bills, Automation

Let's start with the shame, because with money there's always shame.

Maybe yours is the drawer of unopened mail. Maybe it's the subscription you've been paying for since 2021 for an app you used twice. Maybe it's the late fee on a bill you had the money for — you just didn't *see* the bill, because it arrived three weeks before it was due, and three weeks away is *not now*, and *not now* doesn't exist.

If chapter 1 did its job, you already know where this is going. Your money problems are not a character problem. They're a *systems* problem, and the systems that fail you were designed for a brain that experiences time linearly, finds importance motivating, and has executive function to spare. That's not your brain. So we're going to do what we always do: stop trying to fix the brain, and start engineering the environment.

One boundary before we start: this chapter is about the *mechanics* of managing money with ADHD — bills, spending, friction, automation. It is not investment advice, and I'm not going to tell you what to do with your savings beyond "make the saving automatic." For anything involving investments, debt strategy, or taxes, talk to a qualified professional. What I can give you is the layer underneath all of that: a money system that runs even when you're not looking, because you won't be looking. That's the honest design constraint.

## The ADHD tax is real, and it's not a metaphor

There's a phrase in the ADHD community for the money we lose to our wiring: **the ADHD tax.**

It's the late fees. The expired groceries you bought with real intentions. The duplicate purchases because you couldn't find the first one. The parking tickets, the missed-appointment fees, the full-price flight because you booked it the night before, the gym membership from your last novelty binge, the interest on the credit card balance that exists mostly because payments kept slipping your mind.

Individually these are small and embarrassing. Added up over a year, for a lot of us, it's four figures. I want you to notice two things about that.

First: **the deficit story will try to eat this chapter.** "I'm bad with money" is the same voice as "I'm lazy," and it produces the same result — avoidance. When money feels like a moral verdict, you stop opening the mail, because every envelope is a small trial where you're the defendant. Avoidance is where the ADHD tax compounds. The late fee isn't the expensive part; the *not looking* is.

Second: **almost none of the ADHD tax comes from not knowing what to do.** You know you should pay bills on time. Knowing was never the bottleneck. The bottleneck is that the standard system — bills arrive at random times, in different places, each requiring a separate act of remembering and initiating — is a system with a dozen points of failure, every one of which depends on the exact resources we're short on.

So here's the design principle for this entire chapter, and it's worth saying plainly:

**A good ADHD money system requires zero memory, zero willpower, and zero recurring decisions.** If a step depends on Future You remembering to do something boring at a specific time, that step will eventually fail, and the system should be built so that when it fails, nothing bad happens.

## Automation first: fire yourself from the job you keep failing at

The single highest-leverage move in ADHD finance is boring: **automate every recurring obligation, then never think about it again.**

I resisted this for years, for a reason you might recognize: control. Paying each bill manually *felt* responsible. Automation felt like letting go of the wheel. But look at the actual data. My manual system produced late payments a few times a year, every year, forever. The automated version has produced approximately zero. "Feeling in control" and "being in control" turned out to be different things, and I'd been optimizing the wrong one.

Here's the build, in order:

**1. One hub account.** All income lands in one checking account, and all automatic payments come out of that same account. Not three accounts you have to mentally reconcile — one. The ADHD failure mode with multiple accounts is that money is in account A when the bill hits account B, and now your automation has manufactured an overdraft. One hub kills that.

**2. Autopay everything that's fixed.** Rent or mortgage if your landlord allows it, utilities, phone, insurance, internet, minimum payments on every card — *at minimum* the minimums, so a forgotten card can never generate a late fee or a credit hit. If you can afford full-balance autopay on credit cards, that's the gold standard: it converts a credit card into a debit card with better fraud protection, and it makes interest structurally impossible.

**3. Automate the saving before you can see the money.** Whatever amount you're saving — even if it's $20 — have it move to a separate savings account by standing order the day after payday. Not at the end of the month from "whatever's left." There is never anything left; you know this about us. Money you can see is money that's in play. The engineering move is to make it invisible before your novelty-seeking circuitry wakes up.

**4. Build a buffer, and treat it as the system's shock absorber.** Automation on a zero-margin account is fragile — one weirdly timed bill and things bounce. Your first savings goal, before anything fancier, is one month of fixed costs sitting in the hub account doing nothing. Its job isn't growth. Its job is to make sure that when a bill lands early or a paycheck lands late, the system absorbs it silently instead of cascading. Think of it as paying once to stop paying the ADHD tax forever.

**5. Make the invisible visible — on the system's schedule, not yours.** You still need *awareness*, you just shouldn't need it for anything to function. Turn on push notifications for every transaction on every card. This costs nothing and gives you a real-time feed of your own behavior with zero effort. Then put one recurring "money check-in" on your calendar — fifteen minutes, once a month, same day, with body doubling if opening finances alone triggers avoidance (chapter 7 wasn't kidding). In that fifteen minutes you're not paying anything — the robots did that. You're just looking: any weird charges, any subscriptions to kill, is the buffer intact. That's the entire job.

Notice what this design does to failure. In the manual system, failure means late fees, credit damage, shame. In the automated system, if you skip the monthly check-in — and some months you will — the consequence is... nothing urgent. Bills still got paid. The system is built to survive your worst month, not to showcase your best one.

## Impulse spending: don't fight the impulse, tax it with friction

Now the other half: money leaving through the front door at 1 a.m. because a targeted ad found your dopamine system in a weak moment.

Impulse spending in ADHD isn't greed and it isn't ignorance. It's the same interest-driven nervous system from chapter 2 doing exactly what it does. A purchase is a hit of novelty, anticipation, and instant relief — everything our motivation circuitry loves — and modern retail has spent billions removing every particle of friction between the urge and the checkout. One-click ordering is, functionally, a machine built to exploit the gap between our impulse and our reflection.

Willpower advice here fails for the usual reason: it asks you to win an in-the-moment battle, on demand, forever, using the exact regulatory capacity you're short on. You will win that battle nine times and lose it the tenth, and the retailer only needs the tenth.

So we don't fight in the moment. We rig the terrain in advance. The whole strategy is one sentence: **the impulse is a spike, not a plateau — add enough delay and friction, and most spikes die on their own.**

Concrete friction, in escalating order:

**Delete the stored payment details.** Everywhere. Remove your card from the browser autofill, from the shopping apps, from the app stores. Yes, entering sixteen digits is annoying. *That's the entire point.* You are converting a two-second impulse purchase into a ninety-second manual task, and for an interest-driven brain, ninety seconds of boring data entry is often enough for the spike to pass. You've weaponized your own task-initiation difficulty. I'm honestly a little proud of how well this one works.

**Delete the shopping apps themselves.** The mobile app exists to be reachable during your lowest-executive-function hours. The website through a browser, logged out, is the same store with a waiting period built in.

**The 24-hour cart rule.** Anything non-essential goes in the cart and sits for a day (48 hours over ~$100 — pick thresholds that fit your income). If you still want it tomorrow, buy it without guilt — this rule isn't anti-spending, it's anti-*spike*. My own cart archaeology says roughly two-thirds of what goes in never comes out. I didn't resist those purchases. I just never felt the urge twice.

**A fun-money account, prefunded.** Friction alone can curdle into deprivation, and deprivation ends in a blowout — you know the cycle. So flip it: automate a fixed amount into a separate account (or card) each payday that is *for* impulse spending. Anything in there, you can spend on absolute nonsense with zero guilt and zero math. This does two things: it caps the damage structurally, and it removes the moral charge. The question is never "am I bad for wanting this?" It's "is there fun money left?" — a question with a checkable answer, which is exactly the kind our brains handle well.

**Unsubscribe and unfollow.** Marketing emails are impulse-delivery infrastructure aimed at your inbox. Every unsubscribe is a permanent, zero-maintenance reduction in incoming spikes. Ten minutes of unsubscribing beats a year of resisting.

One honest caveat: friction is a dial, not a wall. A determined 1 a.m. brain can re-enter a card number. The goal isn't to make impulse spending impossible — it's to make it *slower than the impulse*, most of the time, and to make sure the times it wins land in the fun-money account instead of the rent.

## Subscriptions, the quiet siphon

Subscriptions deserve their own paragraph because they're perfectly shaped to beat us: one novelty-driven decision to start, then an *infinite series of remembering-tasks* to stop. The business model is, not accidentally, an ADHD tax farm.

Two moves. First, in your monthly fifteen-minute check-in, scan the transaction list specifically for recurring charges and kill anything you didn't actively use in the last month — "might use it someday" is *not now* wearing a trench coat. Second, when you sign up for any trial, set the cancellation reminder *at the moment of signup*, before the dopamine wears off, with the calendar alert two days before billing. Signup-moment You is the only version of you who will ever remember this exists. Delegate to them.

## When the backlog is the problem

Maybe you're reading this with a drawer of unopened envelopes and a feeling in your stomach. A word about that.

The backlog is not evidence you can't do this. It's evidence the old system failed — which we knew. But avoidance has momentum, so borrow structure: do the drawer with a body double, or in one scheduled "worst first" session with a timer and a reward at the end. Sort into three piles only — *needs action*, *file*, *trash* — and action pile first. You'll usually find it's smaller and less catastrophic than the dread suggested; dread is a terrible auditor.

And if the backlog involves serious debt, collections, or money anxiety that's affecting your health: bring in professionals. A nonprofit credit counselor, an accountant, a financial advisor who actually gets ADHD — these are accommodations, exactly like the left-handed scissors from chapter 1. Borrowing someone else's executive function for your finances isn't failure. It's the strategy.

## What "good with money" actually looks like for us

Here's the reframe to leave with. "Good with money," for a brain like ours, does not look like diligence. It doesn't look like a meticulously updated spreadsheet or heroic daily restraint. Those are Windows programs.

It looks like a machine you built once, on a good day, that pays your bills while you sleep. It looks like savings that happen before you notice the money existed. It looks like impulse purchases that die quietly in a cart overnight, and the ones that survive coming out of an account built for exactly that. It looks like fifteen minutes a month of glancing at the machine, and the machine not caring when you skip a month.

Effort didn't build that. Engineering did. And every month it runs, it's quietly writing evidence against the old story that you're bad with money. You were never bad with money. You were running incompatible software.

### 📦 Try this today

Three moves, twenty minutes, in this order:

1. **Turn on autopay for one bill** — pick the one that's bitten you with a late fee before. Minimum payment autopay counts.
2. **Delete your stored card details** from your browser and your most dangerous shopping app. (You'll feel a small pang. That pang is the friction working.)
3. **Put a 15-minute "money check-in" on your calendar** for one month from now, recurring monthly, with an alarm.

Don't reorganize your whole financial life today — that's a novelty binge waiting to collapse. Three moves, then close the laptop. The system gets built one boring bolt at a time, and every bolt keeps holding after you've stopped paying attention. That's the point of bolts.
