# Chapter 11 — Building Routines That Survive the Novelty Cliff

## The graveyard of week three

I want you to picture a specific graveyard. Yours probably looks like mine.

There's the 5 a.m. running habit that lasted nine days. The bullet journal with eleven gorgeous pages and two hundred blank ones. The meal-prep Sundays that produced exactly three Sundays of meals. The meditation app with a 12-day streak, frozen in amber sometime in 2021. The gym membership that quietly became a monthly donation.

If you have ADHD, this graveyard isn't evidence that you're flaky. It's evidence of a pattern so consistent you could set a calendar by it: **the routine dies somewhere between day ten and day twenty-one.** Not day two — you're actually great at day two. Not day two hundred either, because almost nothing survives that long. Week two to week three. Every time. Like clockwork, which is ironic, given our relationship with clocks.

I call this the novelty cliff, and this chapter is about building routines that survive the fall — or better, routines designed with the fall already priced in.

Quick continuity note: everything here leans on machinery from earlier chapters. The interest-driven motivation system from chapter 2 explains *why* the cliff exists. Externalizing from chapter 3, visible time from chapter 4, and low-friction starts from chapter 5 are the raw materials we'll build with. If you skipped ahead to this chapter because "routines" is your sore spot, welcome — it'll still work, but those chapters are the load-bearing walls.

## Why routines die at week two (it's not weakness)

Here's what's actually happening in that second week, and I promise it's mechanical, not moral.

When you start a new routine, it isn't a routine yet. It's a *novelty*. And as we covered in chapter 2, novelty is one of the four fuels your motivation system actually runs on — interest, novelty, challenge, urgency. A brand-new morning routine has all four: it's interesting (new identity! new you!), it's novel by definition, it's a challenge ("can I actually do this?"), and there's often some urgency behind it (a health scare, a new job, a burst of New Year's resolve).

So for the first ten days or so, the routine is *self-fueling*. You don't need discipline. The novelty is paying for everything. This is why day two feels easy and why you privately think, "this time is different." It genuinely feels different, because your brain is genuinely being paid.

Then the novelty wears off. Not gradually — for us, it tends to fall off a cliff. Around week two, the routine stops being "the exciting new thing" and becomes "the thing I do." And here's the brutal part: for a typical brain, "the thing I do" is when a routine gets *easier*, because habit circuitry takes over and the behavior becomes automatic. For many ADHD brains, that handoff to autopilot is weaker and slower. The routine loses its novelty fuel *before* it gains automaticity. There's a gap. You fall into it.

So what you experience as "I always quit" is actually: **the funding ran out before the infrastructure was built.**

Notice what this means. The problem was never your commitment. On day one you were plenty committed. The problem is that you built a routine whose entire energy budget was novelty, and novelty is a depleting resource. Trying harder at week two is trying to run a lamp on a battery you already drained.

Once you see it that way, the engineering question (chapter 1's favorite move) practically asks itself: *how do I build routines that don't depend on novelty lasting — or that refill the novelty on purpose?*

There are three answers that have worked for me and for most of the ADHD adults I've compared notes with. Rotation, shrinking, and anchoring. You'll probably end up using all three.

## Strategy one: rotation — stop trying to marry your routines

The first strategy is the most counterintuitive, because it violates everything the habit books told you: **plan to change the routine before it dies.**

Standard habit advice says consistency is sacred. Same time, same place, same behavior, forever, until it's automatic. That advice is correct — for brains that reach automatic. For us, rigid sameness is often the *cause* of death, not the cure. The routine gets boring, boring means unfunded, unfunded means abandoned, and then we add another headstone and another layer of shame.

Rotation flips it. You keep the *function* constant and rotate the *form*.

The function is the thing that actually matters: "I move my body most days." "I capture my tasks somewhere trusted." "I eat something that isn't beige." The form is the specific implementation: running, this particular app, this particular meal plan. Forms are disposable. Functions are forever.

In practice, rotation looks like this:

- **Exercise:** Instead of "I am a runner now," it's "I move for 30 minutes." This season it's running. When running goes stale — and you'll feel it going stale, that unmistakable "ugh" a few days before you quit — you switch to a climbing gym, or swimming, or long walks with a podcast. Same function, fresh novelty. The graveyard headstone would have read "quit running." The rotation version reads "still exercising, currently on activity number four."
- **Capture systems:** Chapter 3 made the case for a second brain. Purists will tell you switching apps is procrastination. For us, a *controlled* switch every few months — migrating your task system to a fresh tool, or even just a new notebook — can be the maintenance dose of novelty that keeps you actually capturing. The rule: the switch must take under an hour, and the function (one trusted inbox) never lapses during the move.
- **Food:** Not "I meal prep chicken and rice forever" but a rotation of three or four default weeks you cycle through, plus explicit permission to burn one down and build a new default when it goes stale.

Two rules keep rotation from becoming plain old quitting:

1. **You rotate the form, never the function.** Quitting running to start climbing is rotation. Quitting running to start nothing is the graveyard. The question at the "ugh" moment is never "should I stop?" It's "what am I rotating to?"
2. **Rotate on schedule or on signal, not on shame.** The best version is proactive: every eight to twelve weeks, review and refresh. The workable version is reactive: the moment you notice the "ugh," you rotate within a week, before the missed days pile up and the whole thing collapses under guilt.

There's a real loss here, and I won't pretend otherwise: you may never get the compounding mastery that comes from doing one thing for ten years. The runner who rotates doesn't run a marathon. But the runner who quits at week two doesn't either — and the rotator is still fit at fifty. For most of the routines that keep a life running, *continuity of function beats mastery of form*. Save the decade-long obsessions for the things chapter 6 covered — the hyperfocus loves. Routines just need to keep happening.

## Strategy two: the minimum viable routine

The second strategy attacks the problem from the other side: make the routine so small it can survive on almost no fuel at all.

Most of us don't design routines. We design *fantasies*. The morning routine we sketch out in a burst of week-one enthusiasm has six steps: wake at 5:30, lemon water, journal, meditate, exercise, healthy breakfast. It's not a routine, it's a personality transplant. And it's funded entirely by novelty, so we know exactly how long it lasts.

Here's the design constraint that changes everything: **build the routine for your worst day, not your best day.**

Your best-day self doesn't need a routine — she's unstoppable anyway. The routine exists for the day you slept badly, the meds haven't kicked in, the rejection-sensitivity spiral from chapter 10 is humming in the background, and you have negative executive function to spend. If the routine can't survive *that* day, it can't survive, period, because you get a lot of those days.

So we define the **minimum viable routine (MVR):** the smallest version that still delivers the function, small enough that doing it on your worst day is easier than the guilt of skipping it.

Real examples, from my own life and from people I trust:

- Fantasy: "45-minute gym session, five days a week." MVR: "Put on gym shoes and do ten squats." That's it. That's the whole contractual obligation.
- Fantasy: "Journal three pages every morning." MVR: "Write one sentence in the notes app."
- Fantasy: "Full weekly review, Sunday, 90 minutes." MVR: "Open the task list and read it once."
- Fantasy: "Spotless kitchen every night." MVR: "Run the dishwasher. Whatever's not in it can stay where it died."

I know how this sounds. Ten squats is pathetic. One sentence is not journaling. Your inner critic — fluent, as we established in chapter 1, in the deficit story — will call this lowering the bar.

It is lowering the bar. That's the point. Because here's what the MVR is actually for: **it keeps the slot alive.** The catastrophic failure mode for ADHD routines isn't a small session — it's the *broken chain*. Miss two days, and the shame math begins: "I've already blown it." Three days becomes ten, ten becomes a headstone. The MVR makes missing almost impossible, so the chain never breaks, so there's never a "restart" — and restarts, not sessions, are what we're terrible at. Starting a routine from zero costs a fortune in executive function. Continuing a live one, even at minimum size, costs pennies.

And in practice, a funny thing happens: the ten squats frequently become a real workout. Chapter 5 explained why — starting is the expensive part; once you're in motion, momentum is cheap. The MVR is a task-initiation ramp disguised as a habit. But — and this rule is non-negotiable — **on the days it doesn't grow, ten squats counts as a complete, successful execution.** You did the routine. Full credit, zero asterisks. The moment "minimum" starts feeling like "failure," the shame loop reboots and the whole design collapses.

One more sizing test I use: if you've missed your routine more than twice in the past week, the routine isn't too important to you — it's too big. Cut it in half. Still missing it? Half again. You cannot go too small. A one-push-up routine that survives the year beats a P90X fantasy that dies in February, and it isn't close.

## Strategy three: anchoring — stop scheduling, start attaching

The third strategy solves a different killer: even a tiny, well-rotated routine dies if you never remember it exists.

Standard advice says: schedule it. Put it in the calendar. And chapter 4 already told you why floating clock-times barely register for us — 7:00 a.m. is "not now" right up until it's 9:40 and you're somehow in the garage reorganizing cables. A time on a calendar is an abstraction, and abstractions don't fire behavior in a brain that runs on *now* and *not now*.

What does reliably fire behavior? **Things that already happen.** Your day, however chaotic, contains a skeleton of events that occur with near-total reliability: you wake up. Coffee happens (let's be honest, coffee *definitely* happens). You get in the car. You walk the dog. You close the laptop at the end of the workday. You brush your teeth. These are anchors, and anchoring means bolting the new routine directly onto one of them:

> **After I [existing thing that already always happens], I [minimum viable routine].**

- After I start the coffee maker, I take my meds. (The meds live *next to the coffee maker* — chapter 3's externalizing rule: the environment does the remembering, not you.)
- After I pour that coffee, I write my one journal sentence while it's still too hot to drink.
- After I close the work laptop, I do the ten squats.
- After I brush my teeth at night, I set out tomorrow's One Thing on the desk where morning-me will trip over it.

The anchor does the job your prospective memory can't: it *fires at the right moment*, automatically, every day, for free. You're not remembering to do the routine. You're just following the groove that's already there and adding one bead to the end of it.

Three anchoring rules learned the annoying way:

1. **Anchor to events, not times.** "After breakfast" survives the morning running ninety minutes late. "At 7:30" does not.
2. **One bead per anchor.** The moment you chain four new behaviors onto one anchor, you've rebuilt the six-step fantasy morning and it will die on schedule. One anchor, one MVR. Earn the second bead after a month, if ever.
3. **Make the anchor physically point at the routine.** The pill bottle by the coffee maker. The gym shoes blocking the hallway. The journal on the pillow. An anchor plus a visual cue is nearly ADHD-proof; an anchor you have to remember to notice is just a schedule with extra steps.

## Putting the three together (and the streak trap)

Here's how the strategies stack into one design, using exercise as the worked example:

- **Function:** move my body most days. (Permanent.)
- **Current form:** bodyweight stuff in the living room. (Rotatable — review in eight weeks or at the first "ugh.")
- **MVR:** shoes on, ten squats. (Worst-day proof. Counts as full success.)
- **Anchor:** after I close the work laptop. Shoes live next to the laptop. (No remembering required.)

Total design time: five minutes. Survival odds: honestly better than anything else I've ever tried, because every known killer has a countermeasure. Boredom? Rotation. Bad days? MVR. Forgetting? Anchor. Broken chain? The MVR makes breaks rare and the rule below makes them survivable:

**Never miss twice, and never mourn once.** Missing one day is noise. It requires no analysis, no makeup session, no apology to yourself. The only rule is that a single miss triggers extra care the *next* day — usually just moving the visual cue somewhere more obnoxious. What you must not do is streak-count. Streaks are novelty candy that turns toxic on day one of a break; the 40-day streak that dies takes the whole routine with it, because now the routine is "ruined." You're not building streaks. You're building a *default* — a thing that happens on most days, with no memory and no grudges.

One last reframe before the box. When a routine does die — and some will, even with all of this — you haven't failed a character test. You've completed an experiment and collected data: this form went stale at week six; that anchor was less reliable than it looked; that MVR was still too big. Write the finding down (chapter 12 will give it a home), adjust one variable, redeploy. Engineers don't grieve prototypes. They version them.

The graveyard was never proof you can't keep routines. It was proof you kept building them out of novelty and then blaming yourself when the novelty behaved exactly like novelty. Build them out of rotation, minimums, and anchors instead, and week three stops being a cliff. It's just week three.

### 📦 Try this today

Pick **one** dead routine from your graveyard — whichever one you feel guiltiest about. Rebuild it in four lines:

1. **Function:** what was it actually for? (One phrase.)
2. **MVR:** the worst-day version, so small it's embarrassing. Then cut it in half.
3. **Anchor:** "After I ___, I do it." Pick something that already happens daily.
4. **Cue:** put one physical object where the anchor happens, tonight, before you sleep.

Don't restart the old routine. Deploy the four-line version tomorrow, do only the minimum, and give yourself full credit for it. That's not a lowered bar. That's a bar you'll still be clearing in March.
